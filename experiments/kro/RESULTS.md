# Experimental results: simplicity metrics on the kro corpus

We took 232 commits from one author (Ellis Tarn) over two months on his fork of kro. We focused on 11 PRs in detail: 7 Ellis had marked as simplifications in his commit messages, plus 4 we picked as controls of similar diff size (feature adds and bug fixes). For each PR, we asked an LLM to read the touched Go files and produce a design document (a one-paragraph purpose, a list of named procedures, and ten or so testable properties) for both the pre-state and post-state of the change. Then we ran three prompts on each pair of designs. The first asks which design units could be deleted without violating a stated list of preferences (correctness, observable behavior, public API stability, and so on). The second asks whether the design distinguishes exactly the input cases the preferences require, no more and no fewer. The third asks whether the design is a useful summary of the code, meaning a reader could roughly reproduce the code from the design alone. Each prompt outputs a verdict of improved, unchanged, or regressed, plus a one-sentence citation pointing at specific design elements. Good output names specific units and the preferences they serve or violate. Bad output hand-waves, invents design sections that don't exist, or misclassifies code the preferences need as something deletable. Separately, we picked out 12 PRs where Ellis added some machinery and then later removed it in a different PR (we called these "regret pairs"), and we ran Ellis's existing Go static-analysis tool plus its audit prompt on the same code so we could compare what each method catches.

What worked: deriving tenets from Ellis's commit history rather than from a written principles document. He hasn't written a principles document for this branch, but the eight tenets we identified ("correctness over performance," "one name per concept," and so on) can be read directly off his commits. Each tenet has many supporting commits. "One name per concept" appears as eight separate rename commits over the two-month corpus. "Correctness over performance" appears in 15 bug fixes that walked back optimization-layer races. We did not invent the tenets; we named patterns that already repeat often enough in the history to deserve names. The forward-looking detector flags any tenet that conflicts in practice with a higher-priority tenet (for instance, performance optimizations producing correctness bugs), where those bugs are accumulating faster than the underlying conflict is being resolved. Applied to kro retrospectively, the detector would have flagged the performance tenet about 15 days before Ellis actually demoted it at #244. And on one commit in particular (#220, where Ellis split a file the wrong way and walked the split back later in #261), our prompts caught the mistake from the design alone, while Ellis's audit would have justified the original split because no design text said where the file should go. What did not work: the verdicts from S1/S2/S3 depend entirely on the preferences you give them. Under a minimal preference set, every optimization flags. Under the preferences active at the time of the commit, every commit-time addition keeps. The 75% recall and 0% false-positive rate we reported from an earlier matched-controls experiment was an artifact of the preference floor we chose; any rule that flagged anything beyond minimal preferences would have produced the same numbers. The one C1/C2/C3 comparison we ran was on a pair of states (#190 vs #245) where Ellis had already decided that #245 was simpler, so the verdict was foreordained. Compared to Ellis's audit, our framework does a similar job with less machinery. Our prompts catch cases where the design tells the implementer to do more than the preferences require. His audit catches cases where the code itself is too tangled (800-line functions, types doing several unrelated jobs). Our prompts are useful in places where his tool can't run (non-Go codebases, design-only review), and complementary to his audit when both can be applied.

Next: the shift detector and the tenet-derivation method are the two things worth pursuing further; the metrics on their own deliver less than they cost. Three things to do, in order of how much each would help. First, try the detector on a non-kro project to see whether it works elsewhere. Second, ask Ellis to read the eight tenets we derived and tell us whether they match what he has actually been doing, since being wrong about the tenets is the single biggest threat to the analysis. Third, look at how Recursive Language Models (RLM) might apply here. The shift detector and the tenet-derivation method both involve reading across long stretches of history (232 commits, six design documents, and several temporal phases), which is the kind of long-context job RLMs are built for.
