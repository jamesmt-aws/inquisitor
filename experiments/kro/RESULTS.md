# Experimental results: simplicity metrics on the kro corpus

232 commits, one author (Ellis Tarn), two months on his fork of kro. We focused on 11 PRs in detail: 7 Ellis tagged as simplifications in his commit messages, plus 4 we picked as controls of similar diff size (feature adds and bug fixes). For each, we asked an LLM to read the touched Go files at both the pre-state and post-state, producing a design document at each end: a one-paragraph purpose, a list of named procedures, and ten or so testable properties.

We then ran three prompts on each pair of designs. The first asks which design units could be deleted without violating a stated list of preferences (correctness, observable behavior, public API stability, and so on). The second asks whether the design distinguishes exactly the cases the preferences require. The third asks whether the design serves as a useful summary of the code, where a reader could reproduce the code from it alone. Each prompt returns improved, unchanged, or regressed, plus a one-sentence citation pointing at specific design elements. Good output names specific units and the preferences they serve or violate. Bad output hand-waves, invents design sections, or marks code the preferences need as deletable. Separately, we identified 12 PRs where Ellis added machinery and then in a later PR removed it (we called these "regret pairs"), and ran Ellis's existing Go static-analysis tool plus its audit prompt on the same code so we could compare.

Tenets came out of Ellis's commit history. Eight read off the commits directly: "correctness over performance," "one name per concept," and so on. The branch has no written principles document; the commits themselves carry the commitments. Each tenet shows up many times. "One name per concept" appears as eight separate rename commits across the corpus. "Correctness over performance" appears in 15 bug fixes that walked back optimization-layer races. We named patterns the history was already showing us. The forward-looking detector flags any tenet that conflicts in practice with a higher-priority tenet (for instance, performance optimizations producing correctness bugs) when those bugs accumulate faster than the underlying conflict is being resolved. On the kro corpus retrospectively, the detector would have flagged the performance tenet about 15 days before Ellis actually demoted it at #244. On one commit specifically (#220, where Ellis split a file the wrong way and walked the split back later in #261), our prompts caught the mistake from the design alone; Ellis's audit would have justified the original split because his audit demands a design citation and the design did not address file locations.

The verdicts from S1/S2/S3 depend entirely on the preferences you give them. Under a minimal preference set, every optimization flags. Under the preferences active at the time of the commit, every commit-time addition keeps. The 75% recall and 0% false-positive rate we reported from an earlier matched-controls experiment was an artifact of the preference floor we chose; any rule that flagged anything beyond minimal preferences would have produced the same numbers. The one C1/C2/C3 comparison we ran was on a pair of states (#190 vs #245) where Ellis had already decided that #245 was simpler, so the verdict was foreordained. Compared to Ellis's audit, our framework does a similar job with less machinery. Our prompts catch cases where the design tells the implementer to do more than the preferences require. His audit catches cases where the code itself is too tangled (800-line functions, types doing several unrelated jobs). Our prompts cover non-Go codebases and design-only review. Where both methods run, they are complementary.

The shift detector and the tenet-derivation method are the two things worth pursuing further; the metrics on their own deliver less than they cost. Three things to do, in order of how much each would help. First, run the detector on a non-kro project to see whether it works elsewhere. Second, ask Ellis to read the eight tenets we derived and tell us whether they match what he has actually been doing; the tenet list is the single biggest threat to the analysis. Third, look at how Recursive Language Models might apply here. The shift detector and the tenet-derivation method both involve reading across long stretches of history (232 commits, six design documents, and several temporal phases), which is the kind of long-context job RLMs are built for.
