# Experimental results: simplicity metrics on the kro corpus

We tried to detect complexity in code by inferring a design from the code, then asking which parts of the inferred design felt simple enough relative to a list of preferences we wrote in advance. For each change in the corpus, an LLM read the touched Go files for both the before-state and the after-state, and wrote a design document for each version: a one-paragraph purpose, a list of named procedures, and ten or so testable properties. The preferences live in [`prompts/preferences.md`](../../prompts/preferences.md): correctness, observable behavior (status conditions, events), and public API stability (CRD fields and DSL surface). These are baseline behaviors most reconcilers should commit to; we wrote them ourselves. The corpus: 232 commits over two months on Ellis Tarn's fork of kro, all his work. We focused on 11 PRs in detail: 7 Ellis tagged as simplifications in his commit messages, plus 4 we chose as controls (feature adds and bug fixes) of similar diff size.

We ran three prompts on each pair of designs. The first asks which design units could be deleted without violating any preference. The second asks whether the design distinguishes exactly the cases the preferences need. The third asks whether the design is a useful summary: could a reader reproduce the code from the design alone? Each prompt returns improved, unchanged, or regressed, plus a one-sentence citation pointing at specific design elements. Good output names specific units and the preferences they serve or violate. Bad output hand-waves, invents design sections, or marks code the preferences need as deletable. Separately, we identified 12 PRs where Ellis added machinery and then in a later PR removed it (we called these "regret pairs"), and ran Ellis's existing Go static-analysis tool plus its audit prompt on the same code so we could compare.

Ellis already keeps a written tenet hierarchy in his [reconcile-quality skill](https://github.com/ellistarn/home/blob/main/.agents/skills/reconcile-quality/SKILL.md): **Correctness > Performance > Observability > Testing > Simplicity**, with a checklist of about 35 specific items underneath. We derived our own list of eight tenets from his commit history; we saw the written hierarchy afterward. The two lists overlap heavily, which suggests the derivation technique can recover a known-good list from history alone. "One name per concept" appears in both. "Correctness over performance" is his ordering directly. Our "composition over new primitives" matches his Simplicity item "every abstraction earns its existence." Our "architecture legible from import graph" matches his Simplicity item "feature-specific packages compose feature-agnostic packages." We missed Observability entirely; he ranks it third.

What looked like a tenet shift turned out to be the hierarchy doing its job. At kro, the performance tenet was always present, ranked second to correctness. When the controller's performance implementation (per-node resync timers, three hash layers, trigger-scoped walks, content-addressed graph sharing) started producing correctness bugs in late March and early April, the bug fixes piled up. Ellis's hierarchy is explicit: when the conflict is genuine, Correctness > Performance decides. So in late April he split 007-optimizations.md out, marked the optimization machinery "future work, do not implement until core is stable," and stripped the implementation. The detector reads this pattern: when bug fixes for cross-tenet conflicts accumulate faster than the underlying conflict gets resolved, the lower-priority tenet's implementation is heading for the cut. On the kro corpus, the detector would have flagged the optimization implementation about 15 days before Ellis stripped it at #244.

The verdicts from S1/S2/S3 depend entirely on the preferences you give them. Under a minimal preference set, the metrics mark every optimization as deletable. Under the preferences active at the time of the commit, every addition is judged necessary. The one C1/C2/C3 comparison we ran was on a pair of states (#190 vs #245) where Ellis had already decided that #245 was simpler, so the verdict was predetermined. Compared to Ellis's audit, our framework does a similar job with less machinery. Our prompts catch cases where the design tells the implementer to do more than the preferences require. His audit catches cases where the code itself is too tangled (800-line functions, types doing several unrelated jobs). Our prompts cover non-Go codebases and design-only review. Where both methods run, they pick up different things. On one commit specifically (#220, where Ellis split a file the wrong way and walked the split back later in #261), our prompts caught the mistake from the design alone. Ellis's audit would have justified the original split because his audit requires citing design text, and design 005 of that period covered mechanism; file layout was out of scope. The mistake violated his Simplicity tenet (feature-specific packages compose feature-agnostic packages). S2 catches partition-level violations like this directly from the design's structure.

Three things to do. First, calibrate the detector's threshold (how many bug fixes against the lower-priority tenet, over what window, before flagging) so the rule generalizes across projects. Second, apply the framework at PR time on a live project. Catching a forced-cut early is the actual production use; on kro, the detector would have given Ellis 15 days of warning before #244. Third, run the detector across Ellis's other projects (his reconcile-quality skill is the same across them) and see whether other priority pairs (Performance > Observability, Observability > Testing, and so on) produce the same kind of cross-tenet bug-accumulation pattern, or whether the Correctness/Performance conflict is the dominant case.
